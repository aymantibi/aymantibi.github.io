Research

Job Market Paper

  • The Economics of a Trade Policy for Peace?
    Special Mention at IMF MENA Research Conference
    Abstract

    Countries may use trade agreements to promote peaceful relations among allies and advance geopolitical interests. I study the U.S.-fostered Qualifying Industrial Zones (QIZ) agreement, which grants Egyptian firms tariff-free access to the U.S. market conditional on sourcing a minimum share of inputs from Israel. I examine the agreement's economic effects and how the design of its input requirement serves its geopolitical objectives. Using difference-in-differences strategies, I document three findings. First, the agreement generated persistent increases in Egyptian exports to the U.S. and imports from Israel. Second, firms participating in the agreement also expanded exports to non-U.S. destinations. Third, these export spillovers are associated with increased international certification. To quantify welfare effects and evaluate alternative input requirement designs, I develop a quantitative trade model in which firms endogenously choose whether to utilize the QIZ agreement. The quantitative results suggest that lowering the Israeli input requirement increases Egyptian exports to the U.S. and may also increase imports from Israel by expanding firm participation. Thus, stricter sourcing requirements may discourage broad firm participation and thereby reduce the very trade linkages they intend to promote. Yet relaxing these requirements may be geopolitically infeasible, as the additional cost to the U.S. from higher Egyptian exports may exceed the geopolitical gains from increased Egypt–Israel trade.

Working Papers

  • When Trade Agreements Become Easier to Use: Evidence from Jordan
    Revise & Resubmit at Emerging Markets Review: Special Issue on MENA Integration in collaboration with IMF
    Draft available upon request
    Abstract

    Trade agreements often condition preferential market access on non-tariff eligibility requirements, such as rules of origin. These requirements can limit the use of preferential agreements and hinder deeper trade integration. In this paper, I study how trade responds when exporters gain access to a less restrictive preferential trade regime. I exploit the implementation of the U.S.–Jordan Free Trade Agreement (FTA), which introduced an alternative to Jordan’s existing Qualifying Industrial Zones (QIZ) program. Both regimes ultimately provided tariff-free access to the U.S. market, but the FTA did so under less restrictive rules of origin and production-location requirements. Using product-level trade data from multiple sources, I find that the FTA’s less restrictive eligibility requirements affected trade along three margins. Exporters shifted away from the QIZ program and toward the FTA, exports to the United States increased in industries with a strong pre-existing presence in that market, and FTA liberalization induced new product entry into the U.S. market. The results suggest that reducing non-tariff barriers embedded in preferential trade agreements can deepen trade integration by encouraging the use of less restrictive preference regimes and expanding trade at both the intensive and extensive margins.

  • Public Investments and Conflict
    With Ayah Bohsali
    Draft available upon request
    Abstract

    We study how conflict shapes how governments allocate public investments across locations and sectors. We digitize and geocode a novel dataset of capital projects allocated by Nigerian members of parliament to their localities. Using an instrumental variable strategy exploiting distance-weighted spillovers of conflict from neighboring countries, we uncover three facts regarding the relationship between conflict and public investments. First, that public investment decreases with the onset of conflict. Second, that conditional on conflict occurring, the allocation of public investments across regions is increasing in the frequency of conflict events. Third, we show that these increases are concentrated in investment in physical infrastructure. We rationalize these findings through a model where the onset of conflict decreases the returns to public investment. However, as conflict intensifies, governments have stronger incentives to use public investments as a tool to mitigate conflict. The findings highlight how conflict creates diverging incentives for public investment, shaping its allocation across regions and sectors.

Works in Progress

  • When Husbands Lose Jobs or Die: Women’s Labor Supply in Egypt
    With Qinkun Yao
    Abstract

    We study how women in Egypt adjust their labor supply when their husbands experience a severe employment shock, comparing involuntary job loss with the death of a working husband. Employing an event-study difference-in-differences design, we find no employment response to husbands’ job loss but a sizable increase in women’s employment after widowhood, especially among poorer and less-insured households. Husbands’ attitudes toward women’s employment do not significantly affect the magnitude of these responses. The results suggest that women’s labor supply responds more strongly to permanent income losses than to temporary ones, with much of the adjustment occurring through informal employment.

  • Spillovers of IT across Industries and Regions: Evidence from Indian Tech Parks
    With Tanay Agrawal
    Abstract

    The IT sector has become an important driver of economic growth, but its spillovers to the rest of the economy remain less understood. In this paper, we study how the expansion of the IT sector through the establishment of tech parks generates spillovers to the rest of the economy. A simple model highlights two channels: first, productivity growth in IT raises local wages and attracts workers, increasing demand for goods and services produced by other industries; second, when IT services are used as production inputs, higher IT productivity lowers their cost and raises productivity in downstream sectors. We test these mechanisms using two empirical strategies. First, exploiting the staggered introduction of Software Technology Parks of India (STPIs) across cities in a difference-in-differences framework, we show that STPIs increase the number of IT firms and workers in nearby areas and generate broader growth in manufacturing, non-tradable services, and population, consistent with a local agglomeration and demand channel. Second, combining foreign demand shocks for IT services with state-level STPI exposure, we find that manufacturing firms in more exposed states increase their use of computer and software inputs and experience substantial long-run productivity gains. Together, the results show that IT-sector growth can generate broad and persistent spillovers across sectors and regions, highlighting its role in economic development.
